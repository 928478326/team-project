## Project Overview

- **Source URL:** https://q.utoronto.ca/courses/444184/pages/project-overview

### Verbatim Content

Project Overview

It's time to design and grow a Java application using typical industry practices!

Learning Objectives

Design and create code following a layered architecture

Apply design patterns where appropriate

Experience the team software development process using industry-standard tools and approaches

Choosing a Project

You and your team will choose a project from a list of project domains (you can invent your own), create user stories as a team, draw the initial user interface as a team, and then start to grow your project. Everyone will contribute code — you'll each be responsible for implementing 1 or more user stories. Here is a list of project ideas.

Step by Step

To give you an idea of what you'll be experiencing this term, here are the steps that you will be taking to complete your project.

Choose a project domain (one of several examples or your own!)

Identify one or more existing APIs that can be leveraged for some interesting aspects of your program

Create a set of User Stories that describe how the application will be used

Repeatedly:

choose a User Story

figure out how to modify the UI to incorporate it (are there new buttons and text fields and so on? where?)

create a Use Case and figure out the exact user interactions to allow the user story to happen

figure out what new code needs to be written (including unit tests) and write it

submit a pull request

Take part in code reviews to review your team's code

Continually refactor your code to improve the design and adherence to Clean Architecture and the SOLID design principles — applying what you are learning in lectures

Identify future extensions to make your software even better

In short, you will experience the software design process!

Don't worry if this all sounds daunting. You'll have a TA and your whole team helping you along the way! Cross-team discussions, visiting office hours, and being active on Piazza are strongly encouraged!

Some Process Notes

The project will be completed before the end of term with weekly guidance to help ensure your group is making progress. Later labs will be oriented toward making progress on the course project. The primary deliverable will be a group presentation of your project.

Don't be afraid to make mistakes or write badly-formatted/styled code, but expect to fix it later! Refactoring is part of the process and we want to see your team working through the software design process. Yes, you should have a functional piece of software to show off at the end of the term, but more importantly you should be able to:

tell us about its design,

explain why your team chose to design it that way,

describe how you might improve it in the future, if given more time, and

demonstrate effective software development practices, which you will use beyond this course!

By the end of the term, your team's repository should be a snapshot of the design process. Ideally, there will not only be well-documented and well-styled code, a design that follows Clean Architecture, design patterns used where appropriate, and git logs demonstrating significant contributions from the whole team. There may also be open issues left to be resolved, new features you would like to implement, and notes about design patterns you believe could be incorporated in the future.

We encourage every group to make their project their own by incorporating unique features and being creative. You can talk about your unique project experiences in future interviews for internships, so we hope that you make the most of the opportunities this project can provide.

Teamwork and Professionalism

Part of your grade will be determined by how well you functioned as a member of your team. Were you respectful in your interactions with your teammates? Did you act professionally during group meetings? Did you do your fair share of the work? Did you meet deadlines? Did you communicate with your team when you encountered delays? Were you a good team member?

Your TA will be assessing this based on their weekly interactions with your team, peer evaluations, and the contents of your team's git repository.

Please communicate with the course team if you are experiencing challenges or tension within your team that can't be resolved internally. The sooner we hear from you the more we can do to help!

Did you know? 

"Several kinds of people skills were ranked extremely high by the participants—usually higher than C++ and other technical skills. Several participants mentioned that assessing culture fit was what they considered the primary goal of interviews, with technical qualifications being secondary. Technical skills could even be learned on the job."

— quote from What game developers look for in a new graduate: interviews and surveys at one game company

Many of the most important skills for entry-level software developer roles are not technical at all. Instead, they are the kinds of self-directed learning and teamwork skills you will be developing throughout this course project.

As an example, the research article quoted above found that one of the top qualifications for new hires at a game development company was the ability to collaborate effectively and “check your ego at the door”. In other words, being open to feedback, working well with others, and staying humble are just as critical as writing good code.

Team Contract

A team contract formal way to set expectations and norms within your team.

In this course, your team contract will inform you of what you should take into consideration when completing peer and self-evaluations this term.

Team contracts often cover topics such as:

Mode of communication

how will you communicate within your team?

if your team messages you, how quickly are you expected to reply?

Conflict resolution

how will your team resolve disagreements about project direction?

when will you get the course team involved?

...and many more!

Take a moment to reflect on your past teamwork experiences:

What made them successful?

What challenges did you face?

With your teammates, brainstorm a list of criteria that can be used to evaluate how well a team member is contributing to a successful team experience.

Your criteria should be:

Observable – based on visible actions or behaviours

Measurable – quantifiable or assessable

Specific – clearly defined and unambiguous

As an example: Punctuality — Always arrives to lab by ten after the hour.

You can refer to this sample team contract for inspiration.

Next Steps

Have one team member fork the team-project repository on GitHub — this will be your team's repository for your project this term.

Note: If your team already made a repo, feel free to keep using that one.

Add the rest of your team as collaborators.

Complete the TeamContract.md file by answering the provided guiding questions.

Your team should work on a branch that will eventually get merged into main through a PR  — once approved by your entire team.

Once the contract is finalized, each team member should "sign" it by adding their name to the bottom of the markdown file.

Sample Team Contract

1. Purpose of this Contract

This contract sets out shared expectations and commitments for how our team will collaborate during the course. It is designed to promote accountability, professionalism, and mutual support as we learn, complete in-class activities, and develop our course project together.

2. Team Norms and Expectations

a) Communication

We will use __________________ (Slack, Discord, email, etc.) as our primary communication channel.

Team members will respond to messages within ____ hours on weekdays.

All communication will remain respectful, professional, and constructive.

b) Attendance & Participation

Members will attend all scheduled classes and team meetings unless illness or emergencies occur.

If unable to attend, a member must notify the team in advance and provide input asynchronously.

During team activities, every member will contribute ideas and engage in the discussion.

d) Decision Making

Decisions will be made by consensus when possible.

If consensus cannot be reached, a vote will be taken; majority rules.

e) Work Quality

All deliverables should be completed on time, tested, and meet the agreed quality standard.

Members agree to review each other’s work constructively.

Each Pull Request will be reviewed by at least two team members.

3. Conflict Resolution

If conflict arises:

The team will first attempt to resolve the issue privately through open discussion.

If the issue persists, the team will involve a member of the course teaching team as mediator.

4. Accountability

Persistent non-participation or failure to meet expectations may result in lower peer evaluation scores.

All members agree to provide honest and fair peer evaluations.

5. Signatures

By signing below, we acknowledge that we have read, discussed, and agreed to these terms. We understand that this contract is binding for the duration of the course and may be revised with the agreement of all team members.

Team Member Signatures:

Date: ___________________

## Project Expectations

- **Source URL:** https://q.utoronto.ca/courses/444184/pages/project-expectations

### Verbatim Content

Project Expectations

Please refer back to the Course Project page for the high-level description of the project this term.

The project is primarily an opportunity for you to experience working in a collaborative, team environment.

Your team's project is expected to fulfill the following requirements. If your team is unsure if your project will meet any of these, please make sure to ask sooner rather than later so that we can ensure that your project is on track.

Your team must demonstrate the use of Pull Requests throughout the development of your project.

Your team must develop a Java program with a Java Swing GUI.

If your team has a compelling reason to use JavaFX instead, please ask and we will consider approving its use.

Requests to make some kind of web app will not be approved, as there are other courses dedicated to developing such applications.

The scope of your project should align with there being one user story per team member.

Your program must attempt to adhere to Clean Architecture and the SOLID principles.

During your final presentation, your team will be expected to talk about your design and demonstrate the program's functionality. Your team should be prepared to justify any design decisions that you made.

We will talk about SOLID in week 6 and then Clean Architecture in weeks 7 and 8.

Your team must make use of an external API in your project.

Note: it is fine if your team decides to use a Java library that wraps the actual API calls.

The lab in week 7 of the course will discuss APIs.

Your program must demonstrate some form of data persistence.

This could be storing data through some kind of API, writing data to files, or writing data to some kind of database.

Using the API that is used by the user login system example's DBUserDataAccessObject class [updated Nov 7th with version using the correct API url for this term] to store and retrieve a "user" and an associated JSON object could satisfy both of the above requirements for API usage and data persistence. This would be appropriate for projects that rely on being able to share data across multiple users across devices, for example. 

You will each be assessed on your contribution to the team 

Each team member is expected to at least take the lead on one Use Case (I.e., your team can divide up work as you wish, but you each need to write the majority of the code for one use case — after Clean Architecture is introduced, it will be clear what this means).

Please make sure that you are:

Attending all labs and any meetings that your team schedules (or communicating with your team if you can't attend something)

Actively participating on a weekly basis

Contributing an appropriate amount to your team's GitHub repository

We will be checking your team's git logs.

Make sure to give everyone an opportunity to contribute code — this means that you should do your fair share and you should not write code that is the responsibility of another team member.

If we don't see code contributions from you, then we can't give you credit for the project.

If all we see is code from you and nobody else, then we also can't give you credit as it is a group project.

Project Domains and Sample User Stories

For the team project, your team is welcome to develop an application in any domain that your team chooses — but you will need to get the project approved by a member of the course team to ensure the scope is appropriate for the course.

On this page, we have included a few detailed examples of potential projects. These are projects your team can directly choose to work on or your team can go in another direction of your choosing.

Not sure if your idea would make a good project? Ask everyone on Piazza and see what they think! We may even add them to the list below, as it would be great to have multiple teams working in the same domain.

Some general things that make good features for your project and will give you opportunities to make design decisions:

something that would require you to read/write data from/to some kind of file or external location

something that involves well-defined interactions through a GUI

something that allows you to analyze or visualize data related to a domain that your team is interested in

something where one user can create something and another user can then use what was created — like a crossword making/solving program 

Note: You don't have to decide on everything up front — you'll work with your team and your TA will give you some guidance to help scope out your project. More details will be provided, but the general advice as your team brainstorms is to have one user story per member of your team.

Project Example #1: Trivia Quiz Program

As a quiz taker, I want to enter my name so that I can compete against other people.

As a quiz taker, I want to answer trivia questions through a clean and interactive interface so I can test my knowledge in a fun, low-stakes environment.

As a quiz taker, I want to see a progress bar so I know how many questions are left.

As a quiz taker, I want to see my score so I know how well I am doing.

As a quiz creator, I want to use a form to create and edit trivia questions so I can build custom quizzes.

As a quiz creator, I want to be able to preview my quiz so I can ensure a good experience for my audience.

As a quiz creator, I want to save my questions and quizzes so that I can use them later.

As a quiz reviewer, I want to view a summary screen showing each participant’s score after they complete the quiz.

As a quiz reviewer, I want to click on a participant’s name to see a detailed breakdown of their answers and which ones were correct.

As a quiz organizer, I want to share a quiz via a link or code so others can take it.

Depending on the direction your team takes, you may choose to narrow the scope to focus on a subset of these features — just depending on what aspects are most interesting to each member of your team.

Project Example #2: Vocabulary Learning Application

As a user, I want to create an account and log in so my progress and saved words are stored.

As a learner, I want to view a list of vocabulary words grouped by topic so I can study them in context.

As a learner, I want to study the definition of vocabulary words using flash cards so that I can memorize them.

As a learner, I want to take a multiple-choice quiz to test my knowledge of vocabulary words.

As a learner, I want to save words I struggle with to a personal “review later” list so I can revisit them.

As a learner, I want to track my progress over time so I can see how much I’ve improved.

As a content admin, I want to add new vocabulary words and quizzes through a form so learners always have fresh material.

Project Example #3: Pet Adoption Portal

As a shelter admin, I want to add new pets using a form so I can keep the portal up to date.

As a shelter admin, I want to remove pets so I can keep the portal up to date.

As a visitor, I want to browse a gallery of adoptable pets with filters (e.g., species, age, location) so I can find pets that match my preferences.

As a visitor, I want to click on a pet card to view detailed information (e.g., bio, health status, adoption fee) so I can learn more before applying.

As a potential adopter, I want to fill out an application form for a specific pet so I can express interest in adopting.

As a potential adopter, I want to track my adoption application so I know its status.

As a potential adopter, I want to receive a notification or message when the status of my application changes or new pets are added so I stay informed.

Project Example #4: Data Analysis Platform

As a user, I want to import a CSV file through a drag-and-drop interface so I can begin analyzing my data.

As a user, I want to preview the uploaded data in a scrollable table so I can verify that it loaded correctly.

As a user, I want to select columns and apply basic cleaning operations (e.g., remove nulls, rename columns) so I can prepare my data for analysis.

As a user, I want to generate summary statistics (e.g., mean, median, count) for selected columns so I can understand my data.

As a user, I want to create simple visualizations (e.g., bar chart, line graph) from selected columns so I can explore trends.

As a user, I want to save my cleaned dataset and generated charts so I can revisit or share my analysis later.

Project README and Minimum Viable Product (MVP)

The first step of the project is to more formally document a written specification for the piece of software that your team will be designing and building. The below outlines the key information that should be visible in your team's readme. As your team makes progress on your design and implementation, your team should update your readme to reflect your progress. An up-to-date readme will make it easier for your team to prepare your final presentation at the end of the term.

A recommended workflow is to require team members to update the readme as part of any PR that is made.

A template for your team's readme is available here: project-readme-template-2026.md

Use of the template is optional, but please ensure that your team's readme contains all information outlined below.

If you use the template, please feel free to reorganize things as needed.

Additional guidance for what to put in your team's readme is below:

Team or Project Name

Your team can do this now or later, but at some point your team should pick a name. You can go with your group name on Quercus or something else.

Project Domain

This can just be something like "Productivity", "Education", etc...

Including a short blurb about your application is helpful here to give the reader a sense of what your project is all about. 

User stories

Aim for one user story per team member. Reminder that your team can ask for feedback if you are unsure about the scope of your proposed project.

Tip: As you write your user stories, your team may also find it helpful to write a short "specification" of one to two paragraphs — similar to the todo list specification from lab. This can help you perform the typical kind of noun-verb analysis to identify the core entities of your system. This could then go in the "Project Domain" description above.

Use cases

Based on your User Stories, identify use cases required to accomplish those user stories. Each Use Case should follow the template presented in lecture and the course notes. Decide which use cases are part of a "minimum viable product" (MVP) and which can be added later if there is time, once your program demonstrates basic functionality.

An MVP is intended to show the viability of a product — or proof of concept — to potential clients or investors. In this case, your TAs and instructors. It might not have everything working, but it should show off the most important functionalities of the program and demonstrate solid design.

MVP

Each team member should "take the lead" on at least one use case in the minimum viable product. In your readme, your team will indicate who is leading each use case.

"Take the lead" means code most of:

the Use Case Interactor and its related interfaces

a Controller class and Presenter class that are specific to this Use Case

the part of the UI that allows the user to trigger the Use Case

the updating of the UI after the Use Case

"Take the lead" also means coordinate with anyone else working on code that is used as part of your use case. For example, if you are sharing a View with another Use Case, you will work with the lead on the other Use Case to ensure the View works the way you both want it to.

You may find it useful to even start sketching some sequence diagrams for your MVP use cases.

PlantUML is recommended, as you can save the source file in your repo and regenerate any diagrams as you update your design.

Entities for your Domain

Based on your team's chosen domain and user stories, your team should be able to pick out a few entities for your system. Depending on your domain, the number of entities will vary, but there should definitely be at least two. If your team is finding it difficult to come up with entities, you may want to think about ways to expand the scope of your project.

For each entity, indicate its name, instance variables, and relevant methods. This can be in point form or presented as a UML class diagram as long as the information is clearly presented.

PlantUML is again recommended, as you can save the source file in your repo and regenerate any diagrams as you update your design.

API Usage

Document any APIs your team has found that you will be able to make use of in your project.

This should include:

A link to at least one API's documentation.

A few bullet points or a short paragraph explaining how the API(s) will be used in the project.

An indication of whether your team has successfully called each of the APIs listed.

Remember that API usage is a requirement of the project, so make sure to put in some time at the start of the project to explore potential APIs to get a sense of how easy it will be to call them from your Java program. You can refer to the API Advice and API resources pages for ideas. Please ask on Piazza and share if your team is experimenting with a particular API or encountering issues, as others might be working in the same domain and be able to share what they have tried.

Other sections

Your team may want to include additional information, such as documentation on how to run your program or other development notes.

Final Advice

Start early and expect to iterate on your readme several times throughout the project.

Please don't be shy about sharing your draft work on Piazza, as that will benefit everyone. Instructors and your peers will be a great source of feedback as you iterate on your plans. Just toss a link to your repo on Piazza!

Based on user stories that teams wrote on their Week 5 worksheet in class, we put together a list of broad project directions that were mentioned in case any groups are still looking for inspiration: Project Ideas (from Week 5 user stories shared in class)

Some APIs can be more involved to get working, so please make sure to not leave this until late in the project to get working — remember, you can write little snippets of proof-of-concept code any time.

Note: If your team does a good job with your design, your team will still be able to demonstrate your MVP — even if your API isn't fully integrated into your application — but it would be great to see it fully working!

Beyond the above, you'll have to start thinking about design. We encourage you to start thinking about how the user interface might look for your overall program. During the second lab after Reading Week your team will be tasked with fleshing out a design for everything — including sketches of your views and plans for your use cases necessary for your MVP — but your team is welcome to get started!
